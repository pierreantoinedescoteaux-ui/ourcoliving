The collective tax

Charles Durrett and Kathryn McCamant, who brought cohousing from Denmark to North America, have a name for the small irritations of living together. They call them the thorns that come with the roses. Many residents call it the collective tax.

It’s the meeting you’d rather skip. The dinner you join when you’re tired. The colour of the common-house kitchen that wasn’t your choice. The neighbour’s kid who borrowed your bike again.

And here’s what it pays for, as they tell it: shared dinners most evenings of the week, a car you can borrow when you need one, gardens no one could build alone, someone else’s home cooking, and conversations that get deeper every year as you come to know the people next door. Their argument is simple. You can’t have a community without people, and you can’t live with people without a few annoyances.

Muir Commons, the first new-build cohousing in the United States, paid its tax early. The group argued for months over kitchen finishes. A play structure built on part of the vegetable garden split the community, and some friendships didn’t survive it. A few families left over whether toy guns belonged in shared spaces. Years later, a resident told a Quebec newspaper that you can’t love everyone, and reaching consensus takes a lot of talking, but what a wonderful place it is to raise your kids.

What I’ve come to believe is that the tax is part of the gift. Raising something small before it grows, losing a vote gracefully, showing up for dinner when you didn’t feel like it: these are the skills of living with other people, and they make us better at it everywhere else too.

If you’re thinking of living together, ask about the tax when you visit a place. What do people argue about? How do they sort it out? The answers will tell you more than the floor plans.
