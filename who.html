Who it’s for

Who living together helps

When I tell people I live with others on purpose, they often picture students or young founders. That’s part of it. The Quebec cohousing guide makes a longer list, and it moved me: children, teenagers, newcomers, single parents, people living alone, seniors well past seventy-five. Nobody is too young or too old for this.

Children and teenagers

A child in a shared place grows up with a wider circle of trusted adults and other kids right outside the door. Teenagers get something rare: a space between the family home and the street, where they can test their freedom with grown-ups nearby who aren’t their parents.

Young adults

These years are often a time of moving and starting over. Sharing a home lowers the cost of getting started and builds a circle of friends while the rest of life is still settling.

Newcomers

Daily life with neighbours is how you learn the small codes of a new place: how the bank works, where to buy good bread, what people mean when they say “à la prochaine”. The guide says this support often decides how the first years go.

People living alone

About three in ten households in Canada are now one person. A shared place lets you choose your level of company each day, with places to retreat and places to meet, without making you live a collective life all the time.

Couples and families

Couples keep their own home and gain a livelier everyday life. Parents share pickups, meals and the mental load, which the guide notes tends to fall hardest on women.

Single parents

One adult carrying everything can lean on trusted neighbours. Help works when it goes both ways, and in a good shared place it does.

Seniors

Shared places keep older people active and useful, with neighbours close by if health changes. For people over seventy-five, the guide describes a middle ground between living alone and moving into an institution, as long as the design and the support are there.

People on the margins

Designed inclusively and backed by the right partners, shared housing can steady people who’ve had a hard time finding a stable home: young adults leaving youth protection, people in a precarious situation, LGBTQ+ people facing isolation.

What it answers

  • LonelinessThe guide, citing a large review of studies, says isolation raises the risk of dying early by about as much as smoking does. Neighbours who know your name are a quiet kind of health care.
  • Growing oldPlaces where people look out for each other help seniors stay in their own homes longer.
  • The cost of livingSharing tools, cars, gardens and big pots of soup adds up. Cohousing households tend to live in smaller homes and say they’re happier in them.
  • The planetSmaller homes, shared things and fewer cars lighten a community’s footprint.

Source: Guide du cohabitat, Village Urbain (2026), chapter 3, which cites Holt-Lunstad et al. (2010), Statistics Canada (2021 census) and Bamford (2001).

The guide is honest that cohousing has mostly been a middle-class story so far, and many new projects are working to change that through co-ops, non-profits and partnerships. It’s one of the reasons I care about it: done well, it’s a way of living that can include everyone.
