# The Montréal house

> Five people, three years, and the house where I finally designed everything myself.

**Pull quote —** Every room got one job — and the house started doing the work.

## Where it starts   <!-- story.open -->

I live here. That matters to the story: the Montréal house isn't a project I visited — it's the one I designed from the inside, across three years of Tuesday dinners, morning coworking sessions and the slow learning of what a room actually does to the people in it.

The house came with gifts — a heritage building with original woodwork, exposed brick, a turning staircase. Into it moved five entrepreneurs: five people who each needed to focus hard, rest well, and still want to find each other at the end of the day.

## The challenge   <!-- story.challenge -->

Five entrepreneurs living, working and connecting under one roof — without the three needs cannibalizing each other.

And almost no budget. No renovation, no architect, no furniture spree. When you can't build, you have to think — which turned out to be the best design teacher I've had.

## What I did   <!-- story.moves -->

### Every room got one job   <!-- story.moves[0] -->

The work was reorganization, not construction. I zoned the house so each room carries exactly one behavioral job — focus, gathering, stillness, rest — and privacy and community stopped having to negotiate daily.

The stillness room is the clearest example: it's the one room in the house that does nothing else, and that's exactly why it works.

_Method — Zoning by behavior: Give each room one job, and design it to defend that job. When the space has already decided, people stop negotiating over it._

### Working with the bones   <!-- story.moves[1] -->

The building gave me good bones and I worked with them, not against them. The additions were surgical: a long table sized for formal dinners, a rooftop terrace built out for the summer months, light and plants where rest needed defending.

### Programming the year   <!-- story.moves[2] -->

A floor plan becomes a community through rhythm: coworking sessions, pitch nights, Sunday brunches that slid into music jams, yoga, barbecues — some just for the house, some opened to a wider circle.

The rooms set the stage; the calendar made the culture.

## What came of it   <!-- story.outcome -->

The honest metric: people who could live anywhere kept choosing to stay. Three years in, the house still runs — the long table still fills, and the community has outlasted every version of the calendar that started it.

The design patterns it produced became the backbone of the Design for Connection room — tested here first, written up there.

- **5** Residents   <!-- story.outcome.kpis[0] -->

- **3 years, ongoing** Duration   <!-- story.outcome.kpis[1] -->

- **Almost none — on purpose** Budget   <!-- story.outcome.kpis[2] -->

## What it changed in me   <!-- story.changed -->

This house made me trust that space is a tool you can aim: zone the rooms, and the culture follows.

It also slowed me down. Three years of living inside my own design taught me that the best moves are small, patient and mostly invisible.
